Towards an Assessment of Twentieth-Century Philosophy

An undigested intellectual legacy — the long century's search for meaning

In This Chapter — The Five-Phase Roadmap

  1. The Foundations of Continuity — how 19th-century Hegelianism, Empiricism, and Psychologism set the stage.
  2. The Inaugural Breakthroughs — the pivotal year 1900 and the "Big Four" (Frege, Husserl, Wittgenstein, Heidegger).
  3. The Great Divergence — Analytic vs. Continental traditions: shared origins, eventual hostility.
  4. The Evolution of Method — from "atomic analysis" and "verification" to "holism" and "deconstruction."
  5. The Geopolitical Dislocation — the World Wars and the axis-shift from Europe to America.
"No universal history leads from savagery to humanitarianism, but there is one leading from the slingshot to the atom bomb." — Theodor Adorno
CCentral Thesis

The twentieth century is a "long," tumultuous, and unprecedentedly productive era in philosophy, marked by the shift from independent scholarship to total academic professionalization. Its central paradox: it achieved technical breakthroughs in logic and language that transformed the discipline itself, yet it remains haunted by unresolved metaphysical and ethical questions inherited from the 19th century. The century is best understood not as isolated ideas but as a continuous journey of "linguistic turns" — the investigation of meaning, via formal logic or descriptive phenomenology, became the primary route to understanding reality.

§IProfessionalization & Diversification of the Discipline

This section answers: How did philosophy change as an institution in the 20th century, and who entered the profession for the first time?

The twentieth century saw philosophy move almost exclusively into the university setting. The "independent scholar" became an extinct species, replaced by a massive global network of professional academics.

The Entrance of Women

A novel and transformative feature of the century was the breaking of institutional constraints that had excluded women from philosophy.

Key women who transformed 20th-century philosophy

Women did not merely enter traditional areas of philosophy — they transformed the debates themselves in logic, ethics, and social criticism.

The Rise of Specialized Disciplines

New fields emerged that had no clear 19th-century precedent: meta-ethics, philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, and "ecosophy" (environmental philosophy, founded by Arne Naess).

DDefinition — The "Long Century"

Historians call the twentieth century "long" because of the sheer scale of global change it had to interpret philosophically: world wars, genocide, globalization, and technological transformation, compressed into roughly one hundred years.

§IIIThe Inaugural Architects of the Century

This section answers: What did each of the "Big Four" actually break with, and what new method did each install in its place?

Around 1900, several key works "shattered" existing frameworks and launched the century's trajectory.

1. Edmund Husserl and Phenomenology

Husserl's Logical Investigations (1900/1901) was his "breakthrough work."

  • The Goal: descriptively clarify the "experiences of thinking and knowing."
  • The Principle — Intuition: we must accept as evident only what shows itself to be so in "primary grasp."
🧭
Memory Trick — Mnemonic

"Zu den Sachen selbst!" — "To the things themselves!" Husserl's rallying cry. Focus on the essence of the object, not your private psychological feelings about it. Picture a scientist bracketing away every assumption about a flower until only the flower's essence, as it presents itself, remains.

2. Gottlob Frege and the Logical Foundation

Frege is the "posthumous" father of the Analytic tradition.

DDefinition — Sense & Reference (Sinn / Bedeutung)

Frege's breakthrough distinguished a term's Sense (the mode of presentation, or "how" it picks out its object) from its Reference (the actual object picked out).

⭐
Clarifying Example

"The Morning Star" and "The Evening Star" have the same Reference (the planet Venus) but different Senses — they present that same object in two different ways. This is why "The Morning Star is the Evening Star" is informative, while "The Morning Star is the Morning Star" is empty: the sentences share a reference but not a sense.

  • The Context Principle: a word only has meaning in the context of a sentence.
  • Impact: Frege freed logic from "grammar" and "psychologism," treating thoughts as objective entities "extruded from the mind."
"Instead of following grammar blindly, the logician ought rather to see his task as that of freeing us from the fetters of language." — Gottlob Frege

§IVThe Evolution of Analytic Philosophy

This section answers: How did Analytic philosophy's own confidence collapse from the inside, from Russell's atomism to Davidson's rejection of scheme/content?

The Analytic tradition underwent a radical internal critique throughout the century.

Four-Stage Evolution
Stage 1
Decompositional Analysis (Russell/Moore) — breaking complexes down into "simples"
Stage 2
Logical Positivism (Vienna Circle/Ayer) — the Verification Principle: a statement is meaningful only if scientifically verifiable; metaphysics dismissed as "nonsense"
Stage 3
The Attack on Dogmas (Quine) — "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951): the analytic/synthetic distinction is an "unempirical dogma"
Stage 4
The Third Dogma (Davidson) — attacked the "scheme/content" distinction: we cannot separate an "organizing system" from the "data" it organizes
🧸
Memory Trick — ELI10: The Analytic Evolution

Imagine philosophy is like taking a toy apart. First, philosophers thought they could find the single "smallest" piece (Russell). Then they said if a piece doesn't fit a scientific machine, it's garbage (Vienna Circle). Later, Quine and Davidson said you can't just look at one piece — the whole toy box is connected, and you can't separate the "rules" of the game from the "pieces" themselves.

✕Common Mistake

Do not conflate Quine's target with Davidson's. Quine attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction (truth by definition vs. truth by fact). Davidson attacks the scheme/content distinction (the idea that a neutral "content" exists independently of the conceptual "scheme" that organizes it). They are sequential, complementary attacks — not the same dogma restated.

§VThe Shadow of War: Dislocation & Hegemony

This section answers: How did Nazism physically relocate the centre of philosophical gravity, and what did it do to Heidegger's reputation?

The World Wars were not just political events; they were intellectual "dislocations."

  • The Axis Shift: due to the rise of Nazism, the "center of gravity" for philosophy moved from Germany and Austria to the United States.
  • The Flight of Intellectuals: members of the Vienna Circle (Carnap) and the Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer) fled to the US, where they transformed American academe.
  • The Heidegger Betrayal: Heidegger's involvement with the Nazi movement is described as a "great betrayal" of the academy — an attempt at "philosophizing with a hammer" that left German philosophy in ruins by 1945.
!Exam Tip

Questions on this section often ask you to explain why American philosophy became dominant after 1945. The correct causal chain is: rise of Nazism → emigration of Vienna Circle & Frankfurt School thinkers → relocation of institutional and intellectual capital to the United States. Don't just say "the war happened" — name the mechanism (forced emigration of specific schools).

§VIConclusion — The Integrated Legacy

This section answers: Did the twentieth century actually resolve anything?

The twentieth century did not "solve" philosophy; it refined the tools of philosophy to a degree of "immeasurable sophistication." While the century began with a quest for "certainty" and "scientific logic," it ended with a profound "suspicion of grand narratives" (Lyotard) and a recognition of the "plurality of language-games."

Chapter Summary

The twentieth century transformed philosophy from a pursuit of independent gentleman-scholars into a global academic profession — one newly opened to women and organised around brand-new sub-disciplines. Around 1900, Husserl and Frege each launched a method (phenomenological intuition; logical sense/reference) that would define two increasingly hostile traditions, Continental and Analytic, even though both began as reactions against Hegel. Analytic philosophy then ate its own certainties from the inside — from Russell's atomism, through Vienna Circle verificationism, through Quine's and Davidson's attacks on its founding "dogmas." Meanwhile Nazism physically relocated the discipline's centre of gravity to the United States and left Heidegger's legacy permanently compromised. The century closes not with a solved philosophy but with a far more sophisticated, self-suspicious one — still haunted, as the title says, by an "undigested" 19th-century inheritance.

Twentieth-Century Moral Philosophy

From the Naturalistic Fallacy to the Veil of Ignorance

In This Chapter — The Five-Fold Investigation

  1. The Conceptual (Language) — defining "good" and the Naturalistic Fallacy.
  2. The Metaphysical (Reality) — are moral values "queer" entities or natural facts?
  3. The Epistemological (Justification) — how we "know" what is right: intuition, reason, or refined sensibility.
  4. The Moral Psychological (The Will) — the relationship between judgment, emotion, and freedom to act.
  5. The Normative (Systems) — Utilitarianism, Rights, and Contractualism.
"The naturalistic fallacy is neither naturalistic nor a fallacy." — A common critique levelled at Moore
CCentral Thesis

Twentieth-century moral philosophy is defined not by inventing new systems, but by the rigorous professionalization and reconciliation of three classical pillars: Aristotelianism, Humeanism, and Kantianism. The century's progression was driven by a linguistic and metaphysical interrogation of "the good" — moving from Moore's disruption of naturalistic definitions toward sophisticated "sensibility theories" and "contractualism." The period traces a journey from radical skepticism and emotive expression back toward a nuanced, agent-centred realism integrating reason, emotion, and social context.

§IThe Conceptual Disruption: Moore's Challenge

This section answers: Why can't "good" simply be defined in terms of something else, like pleasure or desire?

The century began with G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903), a "broadside" against previous thinkers like J.S. Mill. Moore's primary contribution was identifying the Naturalistic Fallacy.

DDefinition — Naturalistic Fallacy

The error of defining a moral term like "good" by reference to a natural property (e.g. "pleasurable," "desired"). Moore argued "good" is a simple, non-natural property — much like the colour yellow — that cannot be broken down into other properties.

DDefinition — The Open Question Argument

The diagnostic tool for the Naturalistic Fallacy. If "good" meant "pleasurable," the question "Is pleasure good?" would be closed and empty, like "Is a female fox a vixen?" But because the question "Is X (which is pleasurable) actually good?" remains open and meaningful, the proposed definition must be incorrect.

🍬
Memory Trick — ELI10: The Sugar Test

Imagine defining "sweet" as "made of sugar." Someone could still sensibly ask, "I know this is made of sugar — but is it sweet?" Because that question makes sense, "sweet" and "made of sugar" aren't identical. Moore says "good" works the same way: it's its own unique thing that you recognise directly, not a disguised synonym for something natural.

Learning Checkpoint — By now you should know:
  • What the Naturalistic Fallacy is, and who identified it
  • How the Open Question Argument is used to detect the fallacy
  • Why Moore thought "good" resembles a colour term like "yellow"

§IINon-Cognitivism: Morality as Expression

This section answers: If moral claims aren't describing facts, what are they doing?

Following Moore, "non-cognitivists" like A.J. Ayer and Charles Stevenson argued moral language doesn't describe the world at all — it expresses the speaker's internal state.

DDefinition — Non-Cognitivism

The view that moral statements lack truth-value — they are neither true nor false, because they do not describe facts about the world.

Three flavours of non-cognitivism
TheoryKey ProponentCore Mechanism
EmotivismAyer / StevensonMoral claims are "interjections." "Stealing is bad" functions as "Stealing — Blast!" or "Boo to stealing!"
PrescriptivismRichard HareMoral claims are universal commands. "You shouldn't smoke" means "Do not smoke!" — applied to all similar situations
Quasi-RealismSimon BlackburnExplains why we talk as if there are moral truths (using if/then logic) even while only expressing attitudes
✕Common Mistake — The Geach Problem

A major hurdle for non-cognitivism. If "Stealing is wrong" just means "Boo!", then the compound sentence "If stealing is wrong, then encouraging it is wrong" becomes nonsense — you cannot logically embed a cheer or a boo inside an "if/then" structure. Students often forget this is specifically an objection about embedding moral claims inside logical connectives, not a general complaint about emotivism being "too subjective."

§IIIThe Metaphysics of Value: Realism vs. Anti-Realism

This section answers: Do moral facts actually exist "out there" in the world?

Error Theory (John Mackie)

Mackie argued that while we speak as if moral values are objective, we are always wrong. Objective values would be "metaphysically queer" — unlike anything else in the universe, because they would have to be "built-in" with a "to-be-pursuedness."

Cornell Realism (Boyd/Sturgeon)

Moral properties are "natural kinds" or "homeostatic clusters." Just as "health" is a real natural property emerging from many biological factors, "goodness" is a real property emerging from natural facts.

Sensibility Theory (McDowell/Wiggins)

Uses the analogy of secondary qualities like colour. An object is "red" because it is such as to look red to a human perceiver. Similarly, a situation is "wrong" because it is such as to elicit a specific response from a person with a refined moral sensibility — objective, yet grounded in human nature.

§IVEpistemology: Intuition and the "Moral Gaze"

This section answers: How do we justify a moral judgment — through rules, intuition, or perception?

Intuitionism (W.D. Ross)

Ross rejected "grand" single principles like Utilitarianism. Instead he proposed Prima Facie Duties: Fidelity, Reparation, Gratitude, Justice, Beneficence, Self-improvement, Non-maleficence. These are not absolute but "on the face of it" obligations that we intuit; when they conflict, judgment decides which "weighs" more.

DDefinition — Prima Facie Duty

An "at first sight" moral obligation (Ross) that holds unless overridden by a weightier competing duty. Distinct from an absolute duty, which can never be overridden.

Attention and Vision (Iris Murdoch)

Murdoch shifted the focus from choosing to seeing. Drawing on Simone Weil's concept of "attention," she argued morality is a "loving and humble gaze" directed at the infinite complexity of individuals. Art and literature, she claimed, often train this "vision" better than philosophy does.

Learning Checkpoint — By now you should know:
  • Ross's seven prima facie duties, and what "prima facie" means
  • Why Ross rejected single-principle systems like Utilitarianism
  • Murdoch's shift from "choosing" to "seeing," and Weil's concept of attention

§VMoral Psychology: Agency and Responsibility

This section answers: What has to be true of an agent for them to be morally responsible?

The century saw a "renaissance" in Aristotelian thinking regarding the agent.

Three theories of agency and responsibility
TheoryProponentCore Claim
Internal ReasonsBernard WilliamsA "reason" to act must connect to an agent's own motivational set. You cannot have a reason to do something you have zero motivation to do.
Reactive AttitudesP.F. StrawsonResponsibility is found in resentment, gratitude, forgiveness — we hold people responsible because of how we relate to them, not because of deterministic physics.
Frankfurt CasesHarry FrankfurtChallenged the "Principle of Alternative Possibilities": a person can be responsible even if they could not have done otherwise, provided they acted for their own reasons.
!Exam Tip

Strawson's move is often mis-described as "solving" free will. It doesn't — it sidesteps the metaphysical debate by relocating responsibility into our interpersonal "reactive attitudes." If a question asks you to evaluate this move, the strongest critique to raise is that it may only describe how we in fact hold people responsible, not whether we are ever justified in doing so.

DDefinition — The Veil of Ignorance

John Rawls's device: a system is just if we would agree to it without knowing whether we would be rich, poor, talented, or disabled within it.

T.M. Scanlon argued that an act is wrong if it violates a rule that no one could reasonably reject.

🎲
Memory Trick

Think of the Veil of Ignorance as designing the rules of a game before you know which player-piece you'll be handed. You'd design fair rules, because you might end up as the weakest piece on the board.

§VIIConclusion — Reconciling the Three Pillars

This section answers: What was the overall trajectory of the century's moral philosophy?

The century began by tearing down "natural" definitions of morality (Moore), moved through a period of seeing morality as mere emotional "noise" (Emotivism), and eventually rebuilt a sophisticated "agent-centred" ethics. This journey reconciled the Kantian focus on reason and duty, the Humean focus on internal motivation, and the Aristotelian focus on virtuous character and refined perception.

Chapter Summary

Twentieth-century ethics opens with Moore dismantling every attempt to define "good" in natural terms, via the Open Question Argument. Non-cognitivists then denied moral language describes anything at all — reducing it to Boo/Hooray expressions or universal commands, though the Geach Problem exposed the logical cost of that move. The metaphysical question of whether moral facts exist split into Mackie's error theory ("queer" objective values don't exist) versus realist attempts (Cornell Realism, Sensibility Theory) to naturalise or perceptually ground moral facts. Epistemically, Ross's plural prima facie duties and Murdoch's ethics of "attention" replaced any single master-principle. In moral psychology, Williams, Strawson, and Frankfurt relocated responsibility away from abstract free will and into motivation, interpersonal attitude, and reasons-responsiveness. Finally, normative theory evolved from hedonistic Utilitarianism through preference-satisfaction models to Rawlsian and Scanlonian contractualism — arguably the century's most influential real-world export.

Epistemology & Metaphysics of Science
1.1 · 20th-Century PhilosophySource: Blackwell Companion↑ Contents

Philosophy of Science

Induction, falsification, realism, and the "cement" of the universe

In This Chapter — From Discovery to Essence

  1. The Epistemological Foundation — the Problem of Induction, Popper's Falsificationism, Bayesianism, Naturalized Epistemology.
  2. The Crisis of the Unobservable — Realism vs. Instrumentalism, Underdetermination, Pessimistic Meta-induction.
  3. The Metaphysical Structure — Causation, Scientific Laws, and Probability.
  4. Specialized Investigations — Quantum Mechanics, Teleology, and Theoretical Reduction.
"Epistemologists wonder whether we should believe what the scientists tell us. Metaphysicians worry about what the world is like, if the scientists are right."
CCentral Thesis

Philosophy of science is the systematic investigation into the validity of human knowledge and the nature of the reality science describes. It bifurcates into two inquiries: Epistemology (should we believe scientific claims, given the evidence?) and Metaphysics (what must the world be like, if those claims are true?). The field's central tension is the Problem of Induction — the gap between finite past observations and universal future laws — compounded by the challenge of Unobservability, where science posits entities (quarks, viruses) beyond direct human senses.

§IThe Epistemological Gateway: Induction and its Rivals

This section answers: What justifies believing a scientific law will keep holding tomorrow?

1. The Problem of Induction

Science seeks universal laws (e.g. "all sodium heated on a Bunsen burner glows orange"). But these laws are derived via Induction: moving from a finite number of past observations to a general conclusion about how nature will always behave.

  • The Logical Gap: unlike Deduction (premises guarantee the conclusion), induction offers no guarantee — there is no logical necessity that the future will resemble the past.
🦢
Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you've only ever seen white swans. You conclude "all swans are white." That's induction. The "Problem" is that the very next swan could be black, and your "law" would be shattered.

2. Popper's Falsificationism

Sir Karl Popper argued science does not actually use induction. Instead it uses Conjectures and Refutations: scientists propose a theory (conjecture) and then try to prove it wrong (falsification). A theory that survives is "undefeated," but never "proven."

DDefinition — The Demarcation Problem

Popper used falsifiability to distinguish science from pseudo-science (Marxism, Psychoanalysis, Astrology). Science makes precise predictions that could be wrong (Newtonian physics); pseudo-science is so vague that any outcome can be explained away (Astrology).

✕Common Mistake — Popper's Failing

Don't present Falsificationism as a flawless solution. Popper's theory struggles to explain "Positive Knowledge." We build bridges and cure diseases because we believe certain theories are right — not merely because they haven't yet been proven wrong. A purely falsificationist picture cannot easily account for this practical confidence.

3. Bayesianism (Degree of Belief)

Bayesians argue science is about subjective probability. Rationality means updating your "degree of belief" in a hypothesis as new evidence (E) arrives.

DBayes's Theorem

Prob(H/E) = Prob(H) × Prob(E/H) / Prob(E)

If evidence is surprising but predicted by your theory, your belief in that theory should increase significantly.

🎰
Memory Trick — The Dutch Book Argument

If your degrees of belief don't follow the rules of probability, a clever gambler could construct a set of bets against you such that you lose money regardless of the outcome. This is the classic justification for why rational belief should obey probability theory.

§IIThe Battle for Reality: Realism vs. Instrumentalism

This section answers: When science talks about electrons, is it describing something real, or just giving us a useful calculating tool?

1. The Realist Stance

Realists believe science provides an accurate description of the unobservable world (atoms, electrons).

★High-Yield Fact — The "No Miracles" Argument

Science lets us send people to the moon and predict novel phenomena. This would be a "miracle" if the underlying theories weren't at least approximately true — this is the classic Argument from Success for scientific realism.

2. The Instrumentalist Challenge

Instrumentalists view theories as "calculating tools" rather than literal truths.

  • Underdetermination of Theory (UTD): based on the Duhem–Quine Thesis — for any set of data, multiple incompatible theories could fit it. We can always "save" a theory by changing auxiliary hypotheses.
  • Pessimistic Meta-induction: history shows most past theories (Phlogiston, Indivisible Atoms) were eventually proven false. Therefore our current theories are probably false too.

3. Theory-Dependence and Incommensurability

Kuhn and Feyerabend argued that even "seeing" is theoretical.

👁️
Clarifying Example — The Müller–Lyer Illusion

Two lines of identical length appear different because of arrow-shaped fins at their ends. This is a visual proof that our sensory systems already use "theories" about the world to interpret raw data — undermining the idea of a neutral, theory-free observation.

DDefinition — Incommensurability

There is no neutral "yardstick" to compare two scientific theories/paradigms, because the very language of observation changes when the theory changes.

§IIIThe Metaphysical Framework: The "Cement" of the Universe

This section answers: What, metaphysically, makes something a cause, a law, or a probability — rather than just a coincidence?

1. Causation and Explanation

Modern metaphysics of causation began with David Hume's Constant Conjunction: we never actually see a "cause," only one event consistently following another.

The Covering-Law Model (Hempel)
ModelTypeLogic
D-N ModelDeductive-NomologicalDeterministic — "All A are B"
I-S ModelInductive-StatisticalProbabilistic — "Most A are B"

To explain an event, on this model, is to deduce it from a general law.

2. Laws vs. Accidents

Not all regularities are laws. "All the screws in my desk are copper" is a true regularity, but not a law of nature.

  • Systematization (Ramsey–Lewis): laws are the axioms of the simplest, most powerful deductive system of our knowledge.
  • Necessitation (Armstrong): laws are a "natural necessity" — a metaphysical link between properties that goes beyond mere coincidence.
"Laws are consequences of those propositions which we should take as axioms if we knew everything and organized it as simply as possible." — Ramsey

§IVQuantum Mechanics and Biological Purpose

This section answers: Do the special sciences (quantum physics, biology) break the tidy epistemic/metaphysical picture built above?
DDefinition — The Measurement Problem

On the orthodox view of Quantum Mechanics, the "wave function" (a spread of probabilities) only collapses into a determinate reality when a measurement is made. This leads to the famous Schrödinger's Cat paradox: is the cat both dead and alive until we look?

Teleology in Biology

We explain the polar bear's fur by its future purpose (camouflage). Modern philosophers see this as a "disguised" causal explanation: past survival advantage (natural selection) caused the trait to persist — the "purpose" language is shorthand for a causal history, not literal forward-looking design.

Reductionism

The idea that biology reduces to chemistry, and chemistry to physics.

DDefinition — Token-Identity

Every particular biological event is a physical event, but there may be no general physical law that covers all instances of, say, "aggressive behavior" across every different species.

!Exam Tip

If asked to evaluate teleological explanation in biology, don't simply say "it's outdated." The stronger answer notes that teleological language survives in modern biology as a convenient shorthand for a genuinely causal, backward-looking selection story — it has been reinterpreted, not eliminated.

§VConclusion

The investigation of science moves from the Epistemological (justification of induction; realism vs. instrumentalism) to the Metaphysical (causation, laws, probability). Science is not a simple collection of facts but a complex structure of conjectures navigating the limits of observation and the logical gaps of induction. Whether science describes a literal reality (Realism) or acts as a highly effective map (Instrumentalism), it remains the most successful tool for navigating the natural world.

"A single contrary example suffices for a conclusive disproof, but no number of supporting examples will constitute a conclusive proof."

Sartre, Foucault and Derrida

From radical freedom, to power/knowledge, to the instability of language

In This Chapter — Three Movements

  1. The Sovereignty of the Subject (Sartre) — Being-for-itself vs. Being-in-itself; freedom as negation.
  2. The Archeology of Power (Foucault) — from the mind to the "episteme"; madness, criminality, and surveillance.
  3. The Instability of Language (Derrida) — dismantling binary oppositions; différance; the impossibility of pure presence.
"Nothingness must, therefore, exist like 'a worm in the heart of being'." — Jean-Paul Sartre
CCentral Thesis

Late twentieth-century French philosophy pivots from Existential Phenomenology, which grounds the world in human consciousness and radical freedom, to Poststructuralism, which de-centres the human subject in favour of the underlying structures of history, power, and language. While Sartre viewed the individual as the absolute source of meaning, Foucault and Derrida challenged this "subjective" dominance: Foucault revealed identity as a contingent product of historical power regimes; Derrida showed that Western thought's own logic is built on unstable linguistic oppositions that perpetually "deconstruct" themselves.

§IJean-Paul Sartre: The Philosopher of Radical Freedom

This section answers: If consciousness has no fixed nature, what makes us free — and why do most people flee from that freedom?

Sartre's philosophy, primarily detailed in Being and Nothingness, is built on the nature of consciousness and its relationship to the world.

1. The Nature of Consciousness (Being-for-itself)

Sartre adopts Husserl's concept of Intentionality: consciousness is always of something. But Sartre adds a radical twist: consciousness itself is nothing.

  • Transparency: consciousness has no "inside," no soul, no spiritual substance — its entire existence is exhausted by its relation to the objects it perceives.
  • Being-for-itself: unlike a rock, consciousness is self-aware; this internal relation to itself makes it "for-itself."
  • Being-in-itself: the world of objects — "brute, unstructured givens" that are "superfluous" and "absurd," with no intrinsic meaning until consciousness interprets them.

2. Nihilation and the "Worm in Being"

How does meaning enter the world? Through Nihilation (néantisation).

☕
Clarifying Example — The Café

If you are looking for Pierre in a café and he is not there, you do not simply see a room — you see a "not-Pierre" café. You have introduced nothingness into the scene, just by expecting and failing to find him.

DDefinition — Freedom as Withdrawal

Sartre identifies the ability to say "no" or "not" with Freedom. Freedom is the "withdrawal of consciousness from being." Because we are not "things" (Being-in-itself), we are not bound by causal laws. We are, in his phrase, "condemned to be free."

3. Bad Faith and Authenticity

Most humans flee from the "anguish" of absolute freedom.

  • Bad Faith (Mauvaise foi): lying to oneself by pretending one is a "thing" with a fixed nature — e.g. "I can't help it, that's just my personality."
  • Authenticity: the ethical goal — accepting one's status as radically free, and recognising oneself as the "ultimate source of all meaning."

§IIMichel Foucault: The Historian of Systems

This section answers: If Sartre says the individual creates meaning, what happens when we discover the "rules of the game" were already there before we were born?

Foucault represents the "poststructuralist reaction." He rejects Sartre's focus on the individual subject, looking instead at the "objective structures" that shape human experience.

1. Archeology and the Episteme

Foucault uses Archeology to uncover the "epistemic unconscious" — the hidden rules of a time period that dictate what can be said and what counts as truth. In The Order of Things, he argues disciplines like biology or linguistics are not "progressing" toward truth, but merely shifting from one epistemic structure to another.

2. The History of Madness

Foucault identifies two major "breaks" in how Europe viewed madness:

Renaissance
Madness was an alternative mode of human existence — a different kind of "reason"
Classical Age (17th c.)
Madness redefined as "Unreason"; the mad were physically excluded (confinement), seen as having "plunged into animality"
Modern Age (19th c.+)
The mad are brought back into society but treated as "mental illness"; the asylum was not a humanitarian gift but a "gigantic moral imprisonment" designed to control the mad through guilt

3. Power, Knowledge, and the Body

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault introduces Genealogy (influenced by Nietzsche).

DDefinition — Power/Knowledge

Knowledge is not a "pure" pursuit; it is inextricably tied to power. Power generates knowledge about the people it wants to control, and that knowledge, in turn, increases power.

  • Surveillance: modern society moves from the "spectacle" of public execution to a "society of surveillance" — control exercised by monitoring bodies in schools, factories, prisons.
  • The Self-Scrutinizing Subject: in History of Sexuality, Foucault notes we are now controlled by our own knowledge of ourselves. Trying to find our "true" sexual nature, we merely conform to norms laid down by scientific disciplines.
🎲
Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you are playing a game. Sartre says you are the one who decides the rules of the game. Foucault says the game board, the pieces, and the rules were already there before you were born, and they actually decide who you are allowed to be.

!Exam Tip

When explaining Power/Knowledge, resist reducing it to "knowledge is used by the powerful." Foucault's stronger and more examinable claim is that power and knowledge are mutually constitutive — power doesn't just use pre-existing knowledge, it actively produces the categories (madness, sexuality, criminality) that knowledge then studies.

§IIIJacques Derrida: The Reader of Textual Limits

This section answers: If Foucault shows the mind is shaped by external structures, what happens when we discover that language — the very tool we think with — cannot deliver the fixed truths philosophy has always wanted?

Derrida constantly returns to the "body of failed philosophical knowledge" to dissect it.

1. Logocentrism and Deconstruction

Western philosophy is Logocentric: obsessed with finding a "pure presence" or absolute truth (God, The Good, The Self). This rests on Binary Oppositions:

  • Presence vs. Absence
  • Truth vs. Falsity
  • Speech vs. Writing (Speech usually prioritized as "purer")
DDefinition — Deconstruction

The technique of showing that binary oppositions are unstable. The "lower" term (e.g. writing) is often actually necessary for the "higher" term (e.g. speech) to exist at all.

2. Différance: Difference and Deferral

Derrida coined the neologism différance (with an "a") to describe how meaning works. It carries two meanings simultaneously:

  • To Differ: a sign (word) only has meaning because it differs from other signs.
  • To Defer: meaning is never fully "present" right now — it is always put off until the next sign, and the next.
★High-Yield Fact — Pattern Recognition

The "a" in différance is visible in writing but silent in speech. This subverts the very "priority of speech" that Logocentrism assumes — because writing can express a distinction that speech cannot. The pun is the argument.

3. Ethics and Justice

For Derrida, Justice is not the same as Law.

  • Law: a system of rules that is stable and can be deconstructed.
  • Justice: an infinite demand that can never be fully satisfied by any law.

Deciding what is just in a specific moment requires a "leap" or a "madness," because the law can never fully tell you what is right in a unique situation.

§IVConclusion — From the Internal to the External

The journey from Sartre to Derrida moves from the Internal to the External. Sartre finds the "nothingness" inside the human mind that allows for absolute freedom. Foucault finds that this "mind" is actually constructed by external historical "epistemes" and power structures. Derrida finalises this by showing that even our language — the tool we use to think — is a system of shifting differences that can never capture absolute truth.

Continental Philosophy · The Meaning Crisis
1.1 · 20th-Century PhilosophySource: Continental Philosophy↑ Contents

The Gap Between Knowledge and Wisdom

Why more facts have not brought more meaning

In This Chapter — The Historical & Conceptual "Split"

  1. The Ancient Foundation — a unified circle where knowledge and wisdom were one.
  2. The 17th-Century Fracture — modern science demands empirical proof; philosophy becomes an "under-labourer."
  3. The Modern Paradox — as knowledge increases, the "felt gap" of meaning grows more acute.
  4. The Continental Bridge — thinkers who try to reconnect rigorous truth with the "drama of life."
"The eternal silence of infinite spaces fills me with dread." — Blaise Pascal
CCentral Thesis

The defining crisis of modern thought is the widening "meaning gap" between Knowledge (scientia) — the empirical explanation of how the world functions — and Wisdom — the reflective understanding of what it means to lead a good human life. Ancient philosophy integrated these two realms; the post-17th-century scientific revolution relegated wisdom to the fringes, leaving professional philosophy as a mere "under-labourer" to science. Continental philosophy emerges as the primary attempt to bridge this abyss, insisting that philosophy, to remain valid, must tackle the existential question of the meaning of life.

§IThe Ancient Ideal: Philosophy as Formation

This section answers: What did philosophy look like before knowledge and wisdom were pulled apart?

In the ancient world, philosophy was defined as the "love of wisdom." It was not merely an academic exercise but a reflective practice aimed at "forming" human beings rather than just "informing" them.

  • The Examined Life: for Socrates and his successors, "the unexamined life is not worth living." The reverse is also true: "the unlived life is not worth examining" — ancient philosophy was inseparable from the polis, the public realm of political and social life.
  • Teleological Universe: the ancients held a "teleological view," where the cosmos was believed to express human purpose.
DDefinition — Final Cause (Aristotle)

The idea that every natural thing has a goal or purpose for its existence. In this view, nature is a "living book" where theory (understanding) and practice (living) are harmonised.

★High-Yield Fact

The word "theory" descends from theoros, a theatre spectator. This etymology is the hinge of the whole chapter: the ancients were participants living inside a purposeful cosmos; moderns became spectators of a cosmos that has no purpose of its own to offer.

§IIThe Modern Shift: From Wisdom to Scientia

This section answers: What did the Scientific Revolution actually do to philosophy's job description?

Since the 17th century, philosophy has undergone a radical transformation, moving from a practical pursuit of the "good life" to a theoretical inquiry into the conditions of knowledge.

  • The Rise of Epistemology: modern philosophy is dominated by the question "How do we know what we know?" — it becomes the "theory of knowledge."
DDefinition — The "Under-Labourer" Status

John Locke famously described the philosopher's role not as a leader, but as a "janitor" in the "Crystal Palace of the sciences" — tasked with clearing away the rubbish that blocks scientific progress.

  • Knowledge (Scientia): claims must be verified by experiment (e.g. the chemical makeup of water) or they are rejected.
  • Faith vs. Fact: statements about meaning or divinity (e.g. "Jesus is the redeemer") are relegated to faith because they lack empirical proof.

§IIIThe Paradox of Progress and the "Meaning Gap"

This section answers: Why does more scientific progress seem to produce more, not less, existential anxiety?

A "massive paradox": as society becomes more technologically advanced and scientifically "enlightened," the sense of meaninglessness actually increases.

  • Disenchantment of Nature (Max Weber's term): nature is no longer a "world soul" but "sheer, impersonal objective 'stuff'" governed by indifferent physical laws.
  • The Appendix of Wisdom: wisdom is now treated like a vestigial organ — an "appendix we believed we no longer needed," yet it continues to "irritate" us.
  • Luxury and Neurosis: borrowing from Bertolt Brecht ("food first, then ethics"), when humans are "showered with every earthly blessing," they concoct new miseries. The question "What is the point of my life?" returns with a "frightening vengeance."
🚗
Memory Trick — ELI10: The Meaning Gap

Imagine you have a manual that tells you exactly how a car engine works (Knowledge/Science). It's 100% accurate. But the manual doesn't tell you where to drive the car or why you should go there (Wisdom). Modern science gives us the best manual ever written — but we've lost the map for the journey.

§IVThe Two Risks: Scientism vs. Obscurantism

This section answers: What happens when people try to fill the meaning gap the wrong way?

When philosophy fails to address the meaning gap, humans fall into one of two dangerous traps, as described by John Stuart Mill.

RiskCore MoveMill's Label
ScientismAll questions must be answered by empirical science (e.g. reducing life's meaning to Darwinian dispositions)Makes humans "beasts" — stripping away existential uniqueness
ObscurantismRejecting science for "New Age" titles, astrology, or pyramid-holding — a "supermarket of esotericism"Makes humans "lunatics"
✕Common Mistake

Don't treat Scientism and Obscurantism as opposites in the sense of "good vs. bad." Both are presented as equally dangerous failure modes — one collapses wisdom into science, the other abandons rigour altogether. The Continental response (§V) is not a midpoint compromise between them, but a genuinely different move: taking the meaning-question seriously without either surrendering to pseudo-science or abandoning intellectual rigour.

§VThe Continental Response: Reconnecting Truth and Drama

This section answers: Who actually tried to rebuild the bridge, and what did each of them focus on?

The appeal of Continental philosophy lies in its refusal to ignore the "drama of life." It treats the question of wisdom as a necessary condition for all philosophising.

§VIConclusion

The document traces a tragic trajectory: from the Ancient Unity (understanding the world meant understanding how to live), through the Scientific Divorce (nature became a cold, mechanical object), to the Modern Abyss (infinite facts, no meaning). While professional academic philosophy has largely retreated into epistemology and scientism, Continental philosophy remains vital because it persists in the "therapeutic" and "existential" task of bridging the gap between knowledge and wisdom.

Chapter Summary

Ancient philosophy fused knowledge and wisdom into a single teleological worldview, where understanding nature's purposes and understanding how to live were the same project. The 17th-century Scientific Revolution severed this unity: philosophy was demoted to Locke's "under-labourer," clearing conceptual rubbish for science, while questions of meaning were exiled to faith. The resulting paradox is that scientific and material progress has not closed the "meaning gap" but widened it — Weber's "disenchantment" strips nature of soul, and comfort itself seems to provoke new existential neuroses. Mill's warning identifies two equally dangerous escape routes — Scientism (reducing humans to beasts) and Obscurantism (reducing humans to lunatics) — while Continental philosophy, from Hegel through Camus, offers the only sustained attempt to take the question of meaning seriously without collapsing into either extreme.

Key Definitions

Scientia
Knowledge in the empirical, scientific sense — verifiable claims about how the world functions.
Final Cause
Aristotle's idea that natural things exist for a purpose or goal.
Disenchantment
Weber's term for nature losing its "world soul" status under scientific rationalisation.
Scientism
The view that all meaningful questions must be answered by empirical science.
Obscurantism
Rejecting scientific rigour in favour of esoteric or pseudo-spiritual belief systems.
